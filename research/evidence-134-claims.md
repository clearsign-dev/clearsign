# A. Project economics

## https://grapheneos.org/history/

[central] GrapheneOS was started by a single person: Daniel Micay founded it in late 2014 as a solo project, meaning the AOSP-hardening path began with a team of one and has now run for roughly 12 years.
    > "GrapheneOS was founded by Daniel Micay in late 2014. It started as a solo project incorporating his previous open source privacy/security work."

[supporting] The project's earliest technical work was narrow, targeted hardening (a port of OpenBSD malloc to Android's Bionic libc and a port of the PaX kernel patches) plus upstreaming to AOSP, and it progressed quickly only because it was not yet trying to be a production-quality OS.
    > "The project initially created a port of OpenBSD malloc to Android's Bionic libc and a port of the PaX kernel patches to the kernels for the supported devices. [...] The project was able to move very quickly in these days because there was so much low hanging fruit to address and it wasn't yet trying to produce a highly robust, production quality OS."

[central] The CopperheadOS split: a company incorporated in late 2015 became the project's primary sponsor (the OS was called CopperheadOS during that period); in 2018 the company's CEO attempted a takeover, seized the project's infrastructure and donations, and the project rebranded to AndroidHardening and then GrapheneOS. Note this is GrapheneOS's own one-sided account; the page mentions seized infrastructure and donations but says nothing about signing keys or any lawsuit.
    > "In late 2015, a company was incorporated which became the primary sponsor of the project. GrapheneOS was previously known as CopperheadOS while it was sponsored by this company. [...] In 2018, the company was hijacked by the CEO who attempted to take over the project through coercion, but they were rebuked. They seized the infrastructure and stole the donations, but the project successfully moved on without them and has been fully revived. [...] After splitting from the former sponsor, the project was rebranded to AndroidHardening and then to GrapheneOS"

[central] GrapheneOS is currently staffed by multiple full-time and part-time developers funded by donations, with several companies collaborating, and it has committed to never again being closely tied to a single sponsor or company. The page gives no specific developer headcount and names no sponsors or collaborating companies.
    > "GrapheneOS now has multiple full-time and part-time developers supported by donations and multiple companies collaborating with the project. [...] It will never again be closely tied to any particular sponsor or company."

[central] The GrapheneOS Foundation was established as a Canadian non-profit in March 2023 specifically to handle the intake and distribution of donations, i.e. the formal funding vehicle came roughly eight and a half years after the project was founded.
    > "GrapheneOS Foundation was created as a non-profit organization in Canada in March 2023 to handle the intake and distribution of donations."

## https://blog.invisiblethings.org/2015/06/04/otf-funding-announcement.html

[central] In 2015 the Open Technology Fund awarded the Qubes OS Project $160,000 USD, which the founder described as intended to cover roughly one year (October 2014 to September 2015) of development by 3-4 skilled full-time developers (the money was split among Jason Mehring, Whonix founder Patrick Schleizer, and Invisible Things Lab for Marek Marczykowski-Górecki's and Wojtek Porczyk's core work), and she characterized this amount as 'very symbolic' even by open-source standards.
    > "The funding we got is pretty modest to be frank: it’s $160,000 USD with the intention to cover a year of development by, essentially, 3-4 skilled developers working full time, and some even more than full time ;) Even by open source standards this is very symbolic, as other projects, such as e.g. the Tor Project, pay not not much less than this amount to each of their core developers. [...] This funding is to cover the development efforts carried between October 2014 and September 2015 (we have submitted our original proposal back in September 2014)."

[central] Community donations were not a viable funding model for Qubes OS in its first five years: Bitcoin donations accumulated over nearly two years totaled only about $800, which the founder estimated would pay for 1-2 days of a skilled systems developer's work.
    > "And those who think donations or some form of crowdfunding could sustain a project like Qubes are advised to look into the total amount of Bitcoins we have accumulated from donations over the course of nearly 2 years (yes, that’s around $800, for which we could probably afford to pay for… 1-2 days of work a skilled system developer ;)"

[central] Before receiving its first grant, Qubes OS development had been sustained for about five years (roughly 2010-2015) primarily by the founder's private security-consulting company, Invisible Things Lab, together with developers donating their time and, for the Whonix integration work, donations to the Whonix project.
    > "Somehow most people are not concerned about how ITL managed to keep Qubes development over the last 5 years… Whose money have these been, really? [...] the development efforts during that time have been either (1) graciously donated by the above mentioned developers, (2) partly funded by ITL, and/or (3) funded by other means (i.e. donations to Whonix project)."

[supporting] The OTF grant was feature-specific rather than general operating support: it funded porting Whonix (a compartmentalization-aware alternative to Tails) to Qubes OS plus work on hardware compatibility and UX, indicating that by 2015 the Qubes project itself identified hardware compatibility and usability as gaps needing dedicated funding.
    > "OTF does not provide generic funding to projects, but rather sponsors development of a set of specific features. In our case, OTF agreed to support the efforts of porting Whonix (which is a compartmentalization-aware alternative to Tails) to Qubes OS, as well as some more generic work needed to make Qubes OS more usable, both in the context of hardware compatibility and better UX."

[supporting] As of June 2015 the Qubes founder identified the binary build-and-distribution process (theft or coerced disclosure of release signing keys) as the project's weakest security link, stated that building from source via the automated Qubes builder was the only current mitigation, and said deterministic (reproducible) builds were a prerequisite for multi-signature release binaries that would remove that single point of failure.
    > "Admittedly, the binary distribution process currently remains the weakest link here (because it’s possible our signing keys could be stolen somehow, or that one of us could be bribed or tortured or blackmailed to give them away) and this is the case regardless of whether we signed a contract with the OTF or not. Currently the only way to eliminate this weakness is to build everything from sources, which actually is quite nicely supported by our automatic Qubes builder system. In the future, when deterministic builds become reality, the multi-signature scheme on binaries would become possible, reducing the problem of single-point-of-failure with regards of binaries build and distribution process."

## https://www.qubes-os.org/news/2016/11/30/qubes-commercialization/

[central] Qubes OS (launched April 2010) was initially self-funded by Invisible Things Lab from its consulting/R&D revenue, and ITL's first commercialization attempt — selling proprietary-licensed Windows AppVM support alongside the GPLv2 core — failed.
    > "Originally a pet project, it was first supported by Invisible Things Lab (ITL) out of the money we earned on various R&D and consulting contracts. Later, we decided that we should try to commercialize it. Our idea, back then, was to commercialize Windows AppVM support. Unlike the rest of Qubes OS, which is licensed under GPLv2, we thought we would offer Windows AppVM support under a proprietary license. Even though we made a lot of progress on both the business and technical sides of this endeavor, it ultimately failed."

[central] The Open Technology Fund gave Qubes roughly $410,000 in the year before November 2016; the team said this was enough to survive and ship releases but not to execute its roadmap, and judged that amount unrealistic to raise from public donations alone — the stated reason for launching a commercial edition.
    > "I think some kind of measurement is that we’ve received $410k from OTF over the last year. During that time, we’ve got also received some much smaller donations, but that doesn’t change the above figure much. This was enough to survive and release new versions, but not enough to implement everything we’ve planned for. [...] This is a lot of money. And we don’t think it’s realistic to collect it just from public donations. This is why we’re introducing a commercial edition."

[central] After about seven years of community-focused development, ITL stated in November 2016 that it could no longer afford to sustain open-source Qubes development and pivoted to corporate commercial editions (pilot with one to three corporate clients in H1 2017) supplemented by Open Collective donations paid directly to hired developers, with the goal of eventually forming a nonprofit.
    > "the reality is that ITL can’t afford to sustain the open source development of Qubes for much longer. We’re running out of time. [...] we’ve teamed up with Open Collective, which makes it easier to donate to the Qubes project. [...] ITL will not benefit from any of the money donated through Open Collective. Instead, the funds will be paid directly to individual developers who have been hired to work on the open source edition of Qubes. With the help of our community, we hope eventually to build a nonprofit organization that will ensure the long-term future of Qubes"

[supporting] In 2016 the Qubes team explicitly declined to sell dedicated Qubes laptops as a revenue model, citing the difficulty of making hardware trustworthy enough for its 'seal of approval' plus business and logistics challenges.
    > "One possibility that some of our users have inquired about is that we sell dedicated Qubes hardware (i.e. laptops). However, there are a number of challenges here, both in terms of making the hardware trustworthy enough to merit our “seal of approval”, and from a business and logistics perspective. For these reasons, we don’t plan to pursue this option in the immediate future."

[central] The Qubes founders state that a security OS of this kind requires a multi-person core team (the security-research work alone exceeds one person's capacity), and that funding shortfalls produced concrete delivery failures: Qubes 4.0 repeatedly delayed, GNOME support never added, Live USB left in alpha and unmaintained, and many unfixed bugs.
    > "Even if we consider only the “security research” aspect of Qubes, this area alone easily scales beyond the capabilities of a single human being. In order to continue to deliver on its promise of strong desktop security, Qubes must retain and expand its core team, and this requires substantial funding. [...] Qubes 4.0 is delayed more and more, We haven’t managed to add Gnome support, Live USB is still in alpha phase and basically unmaintained, A lot of bugs are not fixed (see GitHub issues)"

## https://discuss.grapheneos.org/d/34369-original-grapheneos-responses-to-wired-fact-checker

[central] As of April 2026, GrapheneOS self-reports roughly 10 full-time paid developers within about 20 active contributors, is hiring more, and is funded entirely by donations (having grown from a single underpaid full-time developer before the 2018 Copperhead split).
    > "Instead of having a single full time developer barely being paid anything, GrapheneOS now has around 10 full time developers and is in the process of expanding by hiring several more. It's entirely funded by donations and is far more than simply being sustainable that way. [...] The number of active contributors to the project have varied over time, with around 20 today, of which around 10 are paid."

[central] GrapheneOS self-reports an installed base of approximately 350,000 to 400,000 users as of April 2026 (its answer to WIRED's question about whether it had ~250,000 users in the early 2020s).
    > "Today GrapheneOS has around 350k to 400k users."

[central] The GrapheneOS Foundation is a Canadian non-profit incorporated in March 2023 with three directors; founder Daniel Micay draws no salary from it (income via GitHub Sponsors), stepped down as lead developer in June 2023 after being swatted starting April 2023, and was succeeded by another long-term contributor.
    > "The GrapheneOS Foundation is a non-profit incorporated in Canada in March 2023. [...] Daniel solely gets his income via GitHub Sponsors and hasn't paid himself anything from the GrapheneOS Foundation. [...] He was the lead developer until June 2023, and remains one of the foundation's three directors. [...] Micay was swatted three times, starting in April 2023. [...] It contributed to Micay stepping down as lead developer in June 2023. Another long-term GrapheneOS contributor took over as lead developer."

[central] GrapheneOS's account of the CopperheadOS split: the hardened-OS project was started by Micay alone in 2014 before Copperhead was incorporated in November 2015; CopperheadOS, Android Hardening Project and GrapheneOS are renames of one continuous codebase; in 2018 Micay permanently deleted the release signing keys and severed ties, which froze existing CopperheadOS builds so they could no longer be patched; GrapheneOS alleges Donaldson took about $300,000 of Bitcoin donations, and a suit for $400k in damages was filed in 2020.
    > "McGrady stepped away before Copperhead was incorporated in November 2015. [...] Eventually CopperheadOS was renamed to the Android Hardening Project, and then GrapheneOS. These were renames, not rewrites or forks - they are all the same project. [...] Micay therefore deleted the keys permanently and severed ties with Copperhead and Donaldson. [...] Micay’s actions prevented a takeover and preserved users’ safety from forced changes, but they also froze the existing CopperheadOS builds so vulnerabilities could no longer be patched. [...] He ultimately ended up stealing around $300,000 worth of Bitcoin donations made to the open source project. [...] We understand that the suit demanding $400k in damages was filed later, in 2020."

[supporting] Sandboxed Google Play was added to GrapheneOS around mid-2021 as an opt-in compatibility layer (GmsCompat) that runs Play services as an unprivileged sandboxed app; GrapheneOS states many users run without it, and identifies its Network and Sensors permission toggles as GrapheneOS-specific additions beyond stock Android's location/contacts/background controls.
    > "Micay's open source project was launched in 2014 and includes many flagship features; sandboxed Google Play was added later, around mid‑2021. Many users run GrapheneOS without sandboxed Google Play. [...] GrapheneOS demonstrates that most privileged access granted to Play Services is unnecessary, by providing an opt-in compatibility layer which receives absolutely no special access or privileges, and provides near complete compatibility with the app ecosystem depending on Google Play. [...] Network and sensors permission controls are GrapheneOS features."

## https://www.redox-os.org/news/this-month-251231/

[central] Redox OS, a from-scratch Rust microkernel OS, marked its 10th anniversary since its first commit in 2025, and as of December 2025 the project still describes Wayland support, hardware-accelerated graphics, self-hosting and running on a smartphone as in-progress or first-attempt goals rather than achieved ones — a direct data point on the multi-decade timeline of the 'build from scratch / microkernel' path.
    > "It was an wonderful year for Redox, as the project marked its 10th anniversary since the first commit. [...] This year saw some big steps toward Wayland support and baby steps toward hardware-accelerated graphics. And a first attempt at running Redox on a smartphone!"

[central] The Redox OS Nonprofit's 2025 cash funding was about $37,000 ($17,000 in community donations plus $20,000 contributed by lead maintainer Jeremy Soller), spent on student Redox Summer of Code projects, a part-time build engineer, a community manager and overhead; it says it is seeking larger foundation/corporate donations 'to help support full-time developers', implying the nonprofit does not currently fund full-time developers. Additional developer time comes from three NLnet/NGI Zero grants covering two key contributors (Unix-style signals, capability-based security — only started summer 2025 — and io_uring-like IO).
    > "We raised $17,000 in donations from our community, and $20,000 from Jeremy Soller that he had received prior to forming the Redox OS Nonprofit. These donations have been used to fund our Redox Summer of Code projects for students, our part-time build engineer, our community manager, and to keep the lights on. [...] As we get closer to having Redox ready for specific use cases, we are looking for larger donations, especially from foundations and corporate partners, to help support full-time developers."

[central] A decade in, Redox's contributor base in 2025 was 'more than 40 significant contributors' plus smaller contributions, with over 1,000 members in its Matrix chat, and it is governed by a four-person unpaid board (Ron Williams as president/chair, Jeremy Soller as treasurer and architect/lead maintainer, Alberto Souza as secretary, Mathew John Roberts as director) — a benchmark for the team size a from-scratch OS accumulates over ten years.
    > "This year saw more than 40 significant contributors to the Redox code base, and many smaller contributions as well. [...] All director positions are unpaid, but being a director does not prevent someone from obtaining funding for their technical work."

[supporting] As of December 2025 Redox's first-ever GPU driver (for Intel Tiger Lake and Kaby Lake integrated GPUs, written by Jeremy Soller) supports only mode setting with no hardware acceleration; the Redox desktop has relied on BIOS VESA / UEFI GOP framebuffer drivers for several years, and the USB SCSI driver was disabled for unreliability — illustrating the hardware/driver gap a from-scratch OS faces versus AOSP or Linux-based paths.
    > "Jeremy Soller created an initial GPU driver for Intel Tiger Lake and Kaby Lake integrated GPUs, after reading many pages of documentation. Currently the driver only supports mode setting. Jeremy Soller and bjorn3 have begun creating ioctl support for hardware-accelerated rendering, but no graphics acceleration is supported yet."

[supporting] Redox publishes daily 'server' and 'desktop' images in a 'harddrive' variant for virtual machines and a 'livedisk' variant for real hardware, and in December 2025 improved macOS and FreeBSD build-host support and extended CI testing to ARM64 and RISC-V via the redoxer tool (including a Docker container for ARM64/i586 testing) — relevant to a VM-first, Apple Silicon-based getting-started workflow for the microkernel path.
    > "If you want to test in a virtual machine use the “harddrive” images. If you want to test on real hardware use the “livedisk” images. [...] (build) Wildan Mubarok improved the FreeBSD and MacOS build support [...] (test) Wildan Mubarok created a Docker container for ARM64 and i586 testing on Redoxer"

## https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GrapheneOS

[central] GrapheneOS had roughly 400,000 active users as of April 2026, a figure the developers derive only from update-server access logs because the OS ships no telemetry; this is the article's sole adoption metric and should be treated as a self-reported, inexact estimate.
    > "As of April 2026, GrapheneOS developers reported they had around 400,000 active users. The figure is an inexact estimate based on statistics generated from the access logs of the update servers. This is the only way for the GrapheneOS Foundation to approximate the number of users, since no telemetry mechanism is included in the operating system."

[central] GrapheneOS is funded through a Canadian nonprofit (the GrapheneOS Foundation, founded March 2023 in Toronto by Daniel Micay, Dmytro Mukhomor and Khalykbek Yelshibekov) that has received very large individual donations: US$1,000,000 from Jack Dorsey's StartSmall (per the Foundation's own claim) and 55 ETH (~US$110,000) plus 150 ETH (~US$300,000) from Vitalik Buterin in July and December 2023. The article gives no developer/maintainer headcount.
    > "The nonprofit GrapheneOS Foundation was founded in Toronto, Ontario, Canada, by Daniel Micay, Dmytro Mukhomor, and Khalykbek Yelshibekov in March 2023. ... According to the GrapheneOS Foundation, he later donated US$1,000,000 to the project as part of his StartSmall initiative. ... Vitalik Buterin, developer of the Ethereum cryptocurrency, made two large donations to the GrapheneOS Foundation in July and December 2023. He donated 55 ETH, at the time worth around US$110,000, and 150 ETH, worth around US$300,000, respectively."

[supporting] GrapheneOS originated from CopperheadOS: lead developer Daniel Micay co-founded CopperheadOS, was dismissed from Copperhead Limited in 2018 after a licensing dispute between the co-founders, continued the work as the 'Android Hardening' project, and renamed/announced it as GrapheneOS in April 2019.
    > "The main developer, Daniel Micay, co-founded and originally worked on CopperheadOS until a schism over software licensing between the co-founders of Copperhead Limited led to Micay's dismissal from the company in 2018. Micay continued working on the Android Hardening project, which was renamed to GrapheneOS and announced in April 2019 as the "true successor" to CopperheadOS, with the goal of restoring the same functions."

[central] As of March 2026 GrapheneOS officially supports only Google Pixel 6 through Pixel 10 (plus Fold, Tablet and 'a' models) because of strict hardware requirements — verified boot with rollback protection and custom-key relocking, a secure element with StrongBox, Weaver, insider-attack resistance and hardware key attestation, OS-controllable USB, Wi-Fi MAC/probe randomization, disabled JTAG, and a minimum 5-year (phone) / 7-year (tablet) OEM update commitment — and its first non-Pixel OEM partner, revealed at MWC in March 2026, is Motorola Mobility, with Razr Fold/Razr Ultra devices planned and flagship devices expected Q4 2026 or Q1 2027.
    > "These include bootloader requirements, such as Android Verified Boot with rollback protection, the ability to unlock the bootloader, and relock it with a custom signing key. A high-quality secure element is required in order to facilitate full disk encryption and storage of sensitive cryptographic secrets. The secure element must provide support for the Android StrongBox key storage mechanism, Weaver API, which handles the throttling of unlock attempts, insider attack resistance, i.e., requiring authentication before firmware updates can be applied to the secure element, and hardware-based key attestation ... GrapheneOS mandates a minimum OEM support period of 5 years for smartphones and 7 years for tablets. ... As of March 2026, GrapheneOS is only officially compatible with Google Pixel devices released between 2021 and 2025, including the Pixel 6 through the Pixel 10, Pixel Fold, Pixel Tablet, and "a" models, due to the specific requirements listed above. ... they will be the flagship devices, expected to appear in Q4 2026 or Q1 2027. In March 2026, it was revealed that this partner is Motorola Mobility"

[supporting] GrapheneOS's distinguishing technical choices per the article are: sandboxed (unprivileged) Google Play instead of the microG replacement used by CalyxOS, iodéOS and /e/OS; heavy use of ARM Memory Tagging Extension (first hardware: Pixel 8/8 Pro, Oct 2023; GrapheneOS support added Nov 2023), which surfaced the high-severity Bluetooth LE bug CVE-2024-23694 in March 2024; hardened_malloc; an auto-reboot defaulting to 18 hours; and an uninterruptible duress PIN that wipes data and eSIMs — the latter figured in a July 2026 US DOJ border-search case. The article does not document banking-app or Play Integrity failures.
    > "This differs from other custom Android distributions, such as CalyxOS, iodéOS, or /e/OS, that replace Google Play Services with microG. ... The first devices on the market featuring MTE-enabled processors were Google's Pixel 8 and Pixel 8 Pro models, released in October 2023. GrapheneOS added support for the feature in November of the same year. ... In March 2024, the team reported that the use of hardware memory tagging helped it uncover a high-severity memory corruption vulnerability in an Android Bluetooth Low Energy system component. The bug was acknowledged by Google and became known as CVE-2024-23694. ... The automatic reboot feature is enabled by default and configured to activate after 18 hours. ... In July 2026, in a first-time high-profile case involving GrapheneOS, the United States Department of Justice accused a man of wiping his phone running on GrapheneOS using a duress screen lock PIN during a border search."

## https://www.techtimes.com/articles/322270/20260730/grapheneos-passes-daily-driver-test-most-apps-work-google-pay-permanently-blocked.htm

[central] Google Pay / Google Wallet tap-to-pay does not work on GrapheneOS and the article states no fix is coming, because Host Card Emulation must be run by a system-privileged service with NFC routing rights that GrapheneOS's sandboxed (unprivileged) Google Play cannot hold.
    > "Google Pay requires Host Card Emulation — the mechanism by which a phone's NFC chip simulates a payment card — to be managed by a service running with system-level privileges and NFC routing rights that only system services can hold. [...] There is no version of this that works. There is no upcoming fix."

[central] In TechRadar's month-long daily-driver test (VPN Editor Rob Dunne, Google Pixel 8 Pro), most major US and UK banking apps worked on GrapheneOS with sandboxed Google Play installed, specifically Chase, Amex, Discover, Wells Fargo, Navy Federal, HSBC, Barclays, Monzo and Starling.
    > "conducted by TechRadar's VPN Editor Rob Dunne on a Google Pixel 8 Pro [...] Major US banks — Chase, Amex, Discover, Wells Fargo, Navy Federal — as well as UK banks including HSBC, Barclays, Monzo, and Starling"

[supporting] Banking apps that only check basic integrity (root detection) pass on GrapheneOS, while apps that require Google's official device certification fail. Note: the article uses the legacy SafetyNet terms 'basicIntegrity' and 'ctsProfileMatch' rather than current Play Integrity API verdict names (MEETS_BASIC_INTEGRITY / MEETS_DEVICE_INTEGRITY / MEETS_STRONG_INTEGRITY), so the terminology should be cross-checked against GrapheneOS's own documentation.
    > "Apps that check only whether the device is rooted (the basicIntegrity check) pass GrapheneOS fine. Apps that check whether the device has Google's official device certification (the ctsProfileMatch check) do not"

[central] As of July 2026 GrapheneOS supports only Google Pixel 6 through Pixel 10 series devices; Motorola and the GrapheneOS Foundation announced a formal long-term partnership on March 2, 2026, but compatible Motorola devices are not expected until 2027.
    > "compatible with Google Pixel devices ranging from the Pixel 6 series through the Pixel 10 series [...] Motorola and the GrapheneOS Foundation announced a formal long-term partnership [...] March 2, 2026 [...] compatible Motorola devices are not expected until 2027"

[supporting] Android Auto has worked on GrapheneOS since late 2023 (earlier versions required extra configuration), and sandboxed Google Play runs the entire Play stack (Play Services, Play Store, Play-dependent apps) inside the same unprivileged app sandbox as ordinary apps rather than with system privileges as on stock Android.
    > "Android Auto, which required extra configuration steps in earlier versions of GrapheneOS, has functioned since late 2023 [...] It runs the entire Google Play stack — Play Services, the Play Store, and any Play-dependent apps — inside the same unprivileged app sandbox as everything else."

## https://android-developers.googleblog.com/2024/12/making-play-integrity-api-faster-resilient-private.html

[central] As of 3 December 2024, Google changed the technology behind the Play Integrity API on all Android 13 (API level 33)+ devices so that verdicts require hardware-backed signals via Android Platform Key Attestation; existing integrations could opt in immediately and all integrations were to be automatically transitioned in May 2025.
    > "Starting today, we’re changing the technology that powers the Play Integrity API on all devices running Android 13 (API level 33) and above to make it faster, more reliable, and more private for users. Developers already using Play Integrity API can opt-in to start using the new verdicts today; all API integrations will automatically transition to the new verdicts in May 2025. The improved verdicts will require, and make greater use of, hardware-backed security signals using Android Platform Key Attestation, making it significantly harder and more costly for attackers to bypass."

[central] The 'meets-strong-integrity' device verdict was redefined to require a security update within the last year on Android 13+ devices, and Google explicitly positions this strong label for banking, finance, government and enterprise apps gating sensitive actions such as money transfers, while recommending a fallback when the strong label is unavailable.
    > "Now, we’re updating the “meets-strong-integrity” response to require a security update within the last year on devices running Android 13 and above. This update gives apps with higher security needs, like banking and finance apps, governments, and enterprise apps, more ways to tailor their level of protection for sensitive features, like transferring money. When the strong label isn’t available for the user, we recommend that you have a fallback option."

[supporting] Google states it will unilaterally adjust Play Integrity verdicts server-side when it detects evidence of excessive activity or attestation key compromise, with no developer action required (relevant to the durability of leaked-keybox spoofing workarounds used on custom ROMs).
    > "We’ll also be adjusting verdicts when we detect security threats across Android SDK versions, such as when there is evidence of excessive activity or key compromise, without requiring any developer work."

[supporting] Enhanced verdict signals (the improved 'meets-strong-integrity' verdict and the app access risk verdict) are only returned for apps installed by Google Play; apps installed outside Google Play receive a reduced verdict covering device, account license and app but without the extra security signals.
    > "For apps installed by Google Play, you can get enhanced verdicts with optional signals such as the improved “meets-strong-integrity” device verdict and the recently launched app access risk verdict (which helps you detect and and respond to apps that can capture the screen or control the device, so you can protect your users from scams or malicious activity). For apps installed out of Google Play and all other API requests, you’ll receive a verdict with information about the device, account license, and app, but without the extra security signals."

[tangential] Google claims the move to hardware-attestation-based verdicts cuts the device signals collected and evaluated on its servers by about 90% and, per its own testing, can reduce verdict latency by up to about 80% (Google's privacy framing of hardware attestation; figures are self-reported).
    > "The transition to the new verdicts will reduce the device signals that need to be collected and evaluated on Google servers by ~90% and our testing indicates verdict latency can improve by up to ~80%."

## https://amosbbatto.wordpress.com/2025/11/02/lineageos-statistics/

[central] LineageOS's official telemetry (stats.lineageos.org) counted 4,254,349 active installs as of October 12, 2025, and this is an undercount because stats collection is opt-out; the project publishes only its top 250 builds, and a LineageOS developer (TimSchumi) told the author that fake accounts have inflated install counts for some builds in the past and may still be doing so. This is the only hard, project-sourced adoption figure among the custom ROMs named in the research question and sets a ceiling for the realistic size of the AOSP-fork user base.
    > "As of October 12, 2025, the project counts 4,254,349 active installs of its operating system, and the real number is even higher since some users and builds deactivate the collection of LineageOS statistics."

[central] The custom-ROM install base is overwhelmingly in the developing world, not among Western privacy enthusiasts: Brazil accounts for 1.88 million installs (44.5%), China for 1.02 million (24.1%), and all of Europe for only 154k (3.6%). The author attributes the Brazilian numbers to businesses refurbishing and reselling old mid-range Motorola phones with LineageOS preinstalled (99.8% of Moto G7 Play installs are in Brazil), i.e. the 'general public' running a custom ROM is largely people who want a cheap working phone, not a hardened one.
    > "There are 1.88 million active installs of LineageOS in Brazil, which represent 44.5% of all installs. Likewise, there are 1.02 million or 24.1% of active installs in China. [...] In contrast, there are only 154k active installs in Europe, which represents just 3.6% of global installs."

[central] Google Pixel devices are a negligible slice of real-world custom-ROM usage: they account for 12.3% of official LineageOS builds but only 0.9% of active installs among the top 250 builds, versus Samsung 37.6%, Lenovo/Motorola 31.9%, Xiaomi 17.6%, and OnePlus 1.2%. The author does note that Pixel is the top LineageOS device in Canada and Japan and that English-speaking users tend to buy Pixels specifically to install ROMs for security and privacy. This bears directly on the Pixel-only device strategy of GrapheneOS: it targets the security-motivated minority, not where the mass custom-ROM audience actually is.

## https://thehackernews.com/2026/08/coldcard-hardware-wallet-flaw-linked-to.html

[central] Coldcard's seed-generation weakness was a build-configuration error, not a cryptographic or air-gap failure: since a March 2021 firmware integration, Coldcard's production config defined MICROPY_HW_ENABLE_RNG as 0, the libngu library checked only whether the macro existed rather than its value, and seed generation therefore used MicroPython's deterministic Yasmarang PRNG (seeded from chip UID and timer registers, with no fresh entropy) instead of the STM32 hardware RNG, letting an attacker who can constrain UID, timer state and prior RNG-call history reproduce candidate seeds offline and check them against public blockchain data.
    > "A March 2021 firmware integration error routed seed generation to a deterministic software pseudorandom number generator (PRNG) instead of the STM32 hardware random number generator (RNG). Block says an attacker who can determine or sufficiently constrain the device UID, timer state, and prior RNG-call history can reproduce candidate output streams offline without accessing the device. [...] Block traced the fault to Coldcard's production config, which defines MICROPY_HW_ENABLE_RNG as zero because Coinkite supplies its own hardware-RNG wrapper. The libngu library checked whether the macro existed rather than whether it was enabled, binding the build to MicroPython's Yasmarang fallback. The MicroPython fallback was initialized from the chip's unique ID and timer registers and collected no fresh entropy after initialization."

[central] Galaxy Research attributed a sweep of 1,196 Bitcoin addresses (1,082.65 BTC, about $70.2 million) in 41 minutes on July 30, 2026 to the Coldcard firmware flaw, and later raised its observed estimate to 1,367.05 BTC (about $88.6 million) across 4,585 addresses in three waves; however, the attribution rests on on-chain pattern analysis (a 30 sat/vB, no-change transaction signature), no attacker has been named, and no public report has reconstructed a victim's seed and matched it to a drained address.
    > "An attacker drained 1,196 Bitcoin addresses in 41 minutes on July 30, taking 1,082.65 BTC worth about $70.2 million at the time. Galaxy Research mapped the sweep and tied it to a firmware flaw in Coldcard, the Bitcoin-only hardware wallet made by Canadian firm Coinkite. [...] No public report has reconstructed a victim's seed and matched it to a drained address. [...] Update: Galaxy Research has since identified two additional suspected waves of Coldcard-related sweeps, raising its observed estimate to 1,367.05 BTC, worth about $88.6 million, across 4,585 addresses. [...] Galaxy also cautioned that its findings are based on on-chain analysis and that it has not computationally confirmed that every identified address was generated with weak Coldcard entropy."

## https://grapheneos.org/build

[central] GrapheneOS's officially supported build hosts are x86_64 Linux only (Arch, Debian bookworm, Ubuntu 24.10/24.04 LTS); macOS/Windows and Apple Silicon are not listed as supported build environments, so an Apple Silicon Mac builder must use a Linux VM or a remote x86_64 Linux build machine.
    > "Arch Linux, Debian bookworm, Ubuntu 24.10 and Ubuntu 24.04 LTS are the officially supported operating systems for building GrapheneOS. ... Baseline build dependencies: x86_64 Linux build environment"

[central] A full GrapheneOS build requires at least 32 GiB of RAM (driven by mandatory LTO+CFI linking of Vanadium/Chromium and the kernel), 136 GiB+ of storage for a standard source sync (90 GiB+ for a lightweight sync), plus 100 GiB+ of additional free space for the build output.
    > "32GiB of memory or more. Link-Time Optimization (LTO) creates huge peaks during linking and is mandatory for Control Flow Integrity (CFI). Linking Vanadium (Chromium) and the Linux kernel with LTO + CFI are the most memory demanding tasks. 100GiB+ of additional free storage space for a typical build of the entire OS for a multiarch device ... 136GiB+ storage for a standard sync with history, 90GiB+ storage for a lightweight sync"

[central] A self-built GrapheneOS release must be re-signed with project-generated per-device release keys (replacing the public AOSP test keys), those keys must be reused for every subsequent build, and they cannot be rotated without re-flashing factory images, which performs a factory reset on users' devices — i.e., signing-key generation and protection is a day-one, effectively permanent infrastructure decision.
    > "Keys need to be generated for resigning completed builds from the publicly available test keys. The keys must then be reused for subsequent builds and cannot be changed without flashing the generated factory images again which will perform a factory reset. Note that the keys are used for a lot more than simply verifying updates and verified boot."

[central] GrapheneOS's update infrastructure is just a static web server hosting signed OTA/factory images plus channel metadata files; a derivative OS must point the Updater app at its own URL in config.xml, because a build signed with different keys pointed at the official server will fail verification and loop-download updates, effectively DoSing GrapheneOS's update service.
    > "GrapheneOS uses a static web server as the update server. The release signing script generates the necessary metadata alongside the release files. You simply need to host these files at the URL configured in packages/apps/Updater/res/values/config.xml. ... Using the official update server with a build signed with different keys will not work and will essentially perform a denial of service attack on our update service."

[central] For VM-based development GrapheneOS recommends the sdk_phone64_x86_64 emulator target (which needs no vendor files), but emulator targets do not receive full monthly security updates nor all baseline security features; production-ready targets are Pixel 6 through Pixel 10a (Pixels 8-10 being the best development devices), and hardware-dependent protections such as memory tagging inherently cannot be ported to devices lacking that hardware.
    > "We recommend using the sdk_phone64_x86_64 target in either the userdebug or eng variant for most development work. ... These emulator targets don't receive full monthly security updates, don't provide all of the baseline security features and are intended for development usage. ... The best development devices are Pixels series 8 to 10. ... Most devices are also missing a lot of standard hardware-based security features we use such as hardware memory tagging so those features will inherently not be possible to port to a device not capable of it."

## https://github.com/GrapheneOS/AttestationServer

[central] GrapheneOS's remote attestation service (attestation.app) is published as MIT-licensed open source that a third-party OS project can self-host, but a self-hoster must fork the code to change the server domain, the Auditor app signing key and app ID, and must run a headless Java 25 runtime behind nginx with a systemd unit and SQLite — i.e., an attestation service is bound to a project's own signing keys and domain from day one. The GitHub page/API (fetched 2026-09-16) shows the repo was created 2018-03-17, has ~2,195 commits, 166 stars, 60 forks, 22 open issues, and its latest commit ('update Bouncy Castle to 1.86') is dated 2026-09-14, indicating continuous maintenance of this one infrastructure component for 8.5 years.
    > "Install a headless Java 25 runtime environment. The package name on Debian-based distributions is `openjdk-25-jre-headless` or `jre-openjdk-headless` on Arch Linux. [...] On your development machine, you will need to change the `remote` variable in the deploy scripts to your server. You'll also need to change the DOMAIN in AttestationServer.java to your server and the app signing key and app ID."

[central] Hardware-backed attestation of an alternative OS is only possible if that OS's verified boot key is enrolled in both the Auditor app and the Attestation Server, and the project states that most alternative operating systems lack full verified boot and most devices do not support verified boot with a custom key — the core technical reason a new privacy OS cannot offer GrapheneOS-style attestation on arbitrary hardware. (Quote is from the repo's static/about.html, which the README's first line points to as the project overview at https://attestation.app/about.)
    > "Alternative operating systems need their verified boot key included in the Auditor app and Attestation Server. The app and service display the name of the operating system being verified on the device. Unfortunately, most alternative operating systems lack support for full verified boot and most devices don't support using verified boot with a custom key."

[supporting] As of the most recent stable release, the attestation service can verify only Google Pixel devices — 21 models from Pixel 6 through Pixel 10a (6, 6 Pro, 6a, 7, 7 Pro, 7a, Tablet, Fold, 8, 8 Pro, 8a, 9, 9 Pro, 9 Pro XL, 9 Pro Fold, 9a, 10, 10 Pro, 10 Pro XL, 10 Pro Fold, 10a), all of which are listed as having a StrongBox HSM and the attest-key feature; any Android 13+ device can act as the verifier, but only devices launched with Android 8.0+ can be verified, and each model must be explicitly integrated into the app. GrapheneOS is the only alternative OS listed as verifiable, on the same Pixel models.
    > "Any device with Android 13 or higher can run the Auditor app and use it to verify other devices. However, only devices launched with Android 8.0 or later have the necessary hardware support for being verified. Each device model also needs to be explicitly integrated into the app. The following devices are currently supported by the most recent stable release: Google Pixel 6 [...] Google Pixel 10a"

[supporting] The attestation protocol keeps its keys in a dedicated hardware security module (StrongBox Keymaster, e.g., Titan M on Pixels, available on devices shipping with Android 9+) rather than the main-processor TEE, which the project claims yields substantial attack-surface reduction — relevant to a crypto-key-management OS niche that would rely on StrongBox/secure-element key storage.
    > "Devices shipping with Android 9 or later may ship a StrongBox Keymaster implementation, allowing the Auditor app to keep the keys used by the attestation protocol in the dedicated Hardware Security Module (HSM) (such as the Titan M in Pixel devices) rather than using the Trusted Execution Environment (TEE) on the main processor. This can provide substantial attack surface reduction."

[supporting] The system uses a Trust-On-First-Use pairing model between an Auditor (another phone or the attestation.app service) and an Auditee, with the remote service performing automated verification on a schedule and sending email alerts; the server-side API issues a single-use challenge that expires in 1 minute, and the QR code carries '<domain> <userId> <subscribeKey> <verifyInterval>' in plain text. Email alerts require SMTPS only (STARTTLS is deliberately unsupported).
    > "It performs a pairing process between the device performing verification (Auditor) and the device being verified (Auditee) to implement a Trust On First Use (TOFU) model. The device performing verification can either be another Android device running the app in the Auditor mode or the https://attestation.app/ service for automated verification on a regular schedule with support for email alerts. [README:] The challenge can only be used once and expires in 1 minute."

## https://www.androidauthority.com/android-risk-based-security-updates-3597466/

[central] Google has switched Android's security patch release strategy to a 'Risk-Based Update System' (RBUS): monthly Android Security Bulletins now carry only 'high-risk' vulnerabilities (actively exploited or part of a known exploit chain), while the majority of security fixes ship in quarterly bulletins in March, June, September and December.
    > "Instead of bundling all available security patches into the next ASB, Google now prioritizes shipping only “high-risk” vulnerabilities in its monthly releases. The majority of security fixes, meanwhile, will be shipped in quarterly ASBs. Google defines “high-risk” vulnerabilities as issues that are crucial to address immediately, such as those under active exploitation or that are part of a known exploit chain."

[central] Google no longer publishes source code for the monthly security updates, only for the quarterly ones, which (combined with other AOSP source delays) means most custom ROMs / AOSP forks can no longer ship monthly security updates.
    > "Another downside is that Google is no longer releasing the source code for monthly security updates, only for quarterly ones. This, in conjunction with other delays in OS source code, means most custom ROMs can’t ship monthly updates anymore."

[supporting] GrapheneOS publicly criticized RBUS because OEMs now receive several months (rather than one month) of advance notice of vulnerability details via the private ASB, which is accessible to tens of thousands of engineers across dozens of companies, widening the window in which leaked details could be exploited before patches are widely available.
    > "There is one potential downside to the Risk-Based Update System, as highlighted by the folks behind GrapheneOS, a privacy and security-oriented fork of AOSP. In the past, Google gave OEMs a one-month heads-up. Now, they receive several months of advance notice for the larger quarterly updates. ... While the private ASB is shared securely, it’s accessible to tens of thousands of engineers across dozens of companies, making it conceivable that details could leak to malicious third parties."

[supporting] The July 2025 Android Security Bulletin was the first of 120 bulletins ever to list zero vulnerabilities, the August 2025 bulletin listed six, and the September 2025 (quarterly) bulletin listed 119 vulnerabilities — reflecting the new cadence rather than an absence of bugs.
    > "This explains why the September 2025 ASB listed a staggering 119 vulnerabilities, compared to the zero and six listed in the July and August 2025 bulletins, respectively."

[supporting] The Android Security Bulletin exists in a public version (published monthly since August 2015, generally on the first Monday of the month) and a private version distributed to OEMs and chipset vendors roughly 30 days ahead of public disclosure — a partner-only lead time that independent AOSP forks without partner status do not get.
    > "There are two versions of the ASB: a public and a private one. The public ASB has been published every month since August 2015 and generally goes live on the first Monday of the month. The private ASB, on the other hand, is distributed to OEMs and chipset vendors approximately 30 days in advance, providing them with essential lead time to merge and test the patches before they’re publicly disclosed."
